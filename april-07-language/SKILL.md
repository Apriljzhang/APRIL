# april-07-language

## Scope control (mandatory)

Read `../references/core/operating-contract.md` before acting. Edit only the supplied or explicitly named text, section, or file. Do not add new arguments, evidence, sections, or a manuscript-wide rewrite unless requested.

Use `../references/core/manuscript-contract.md` to preserve locked terminology and claims, and `../references/evidence/evidence-integrity.md` whenever editing citations, quotations, data, or findings.

Edit substantive prose after its argument and evidence are stable. Treat naturalness, cohesion, and disciplinary fit as one language task, not as a separate humanizer pass.

## Priority order

Apply these priorities in order. A later preference must not damage an earlier one.

1. Preserve factual meaning, data, citations, analytic claims, participant quotations, RQs, hypotheses, theme names, and statistical direction.
2. Preserve field-standard constructs and use each construct consistently.
3. Match the target journal, article section, research design, and author's established voice.
4. Maintain calibrated hedging and the limits warranted by the design. State the strongest warranted claim, qualify it once where the limitation bears on that claim, and consolidate study-level limitations rather than scattering generic caution.
5. Strengthen cohesion, information flow, paragraph movement, and sentence rhythm.
6. Remove formulaic, inflated, repetitive, mechanical, or generic prose.
7. Apply APRIL house style unless the journal or user specifies otherwise.

Never invent a citation, statistic, participant detail, educational context, theory, or methodological step to make prose sound more specific.

## House style

- Use British English spelling and punctuation unless the target journal requires another variety.
- Avoid habitual corrective contrasts built as "not X but Y", including "not merely X but Y", "not just X but Y", and automatic "not only X but also Y" constructions. State the intended claim directly or express the relationship without staged correction.
- Avoid paired em-dash interruptions built as `main clause — inserted explanation — continuation`, especially when the inserted text introduces a definition, qualification, example, or contrast. Rebuild the sentence so the information belongs in the main syntax; do not merely replace both dashes with commas.
- An occasional single em dash may remain when the target journal and author's established style permit it and a strong break is genuinely needed. The problem is the repeated clause-insertion pattern, not the punctuation mark in isolation.
- Prefer concrete verbs and specific nouns over vague academic padding.
- Build confidence from explicit claims, evidence, and warrants rather than from unqualified certainty. Avoid apologetic or defensive meta-discourse that repeatedly withdraws a supported claim.
- Use sentence case for headings unless the journal requires title case.
- Keep abbreviations, capitalisation, statistical notation, group names, and construct labels consistent.
- Permit first person when it clarifies researcher action, reflexivity, or argument and the journal allows it.
- Use active voice for transparent decisions; use passive voice when the process or object is genuinely the focus.

## Natural language dynamics

Natural academic prose depends on logical movement, not decorative vocabulary or random sentence variation.

### Empirical narrative

Read `../references/rhetoric/empirical-storytelling.md` when editing an empirical article for research-story depth. Preserve the article's evidence status while strengthening the progression from phenomenon or puzzle to evidence, bounded explanation, conditions, and contribution. Narrative depth comes from visible reasoning, not dramatic questions, promotional language, fabricated surprises, or untested mechanisms.

### Sentence bridging

Apply `../references/rhetoric/sentence-bridging.md` at every sentence boundary:

1. Reopen a concept from the previous sentence as given information.
2. Use a precise demonstrative phrase such as *this distinction*, *these findings*, or *this account*.
3. Preserve a real connective when the relationship is addition, contrast, consequence, concession, or sequence.
4. Combine tightly related clauses with a semicolon, conjunction, or relative clause when separation would make the prose choppy.

Diagnostic: does the next sentence begin from information the reader already has before adding something new?

### Paragraph movement

Check each paragraph:

1. Does the opening establish the paragraph's purpose or claim?
2. Does each sentence develop, evidence, qualify, or connect to that claim?
3. Does old information lead into new information?
4. Does the final sentence complete the paragraph's work rather than repeat it?

Do not enforce "one idea per paragraph" so rigidly that evidence, explanation, and qualification become detached from the claim.

### Sentence rhythm

Vary sentence length and structure when the argument benefits. Avoid a paragraph in which every sentence has the same length and subject-verb opening, but do not chase numerical rhythm targets or AI-detector scores.

Useful revisions include combining related short sentences, splitting an overloaded sentence, moving a contextual phrase, or changing a repeated opening. Preserve cohesion and disciplinary clarity.

## Academic moves

Read `../references/rhetoric/manchester-phrasebank.md` for introducing work, referring to sources, describing methods, reporting results, discussing findings, drawing conclusions, being critical, being cautious, comparing, explaining causality, and signalling transitions.

Use Phrasebank stems as rhetorical tools, not templates. A common academic phrase is not a problem when it accurately performs a needed function. Adapt one useful stem and continue in the author's own voice.

For Discussion prose, also use `../references/rhetoric/discussion-moves.md`. The Discussion guide controls move order; the Phrasebank supplies a wider rhetorical repertoire.

Read `../references/rhetoric/confident-qualification.md` when prose is over-defensive, repeats generic caveats, disperses limitations across the manuscript, or must learn rhetorical logic from an exemplar paper without copying its language.

## Domain calibration

Assume that the manuscript may concern learners, teachers, teacher educators, classrooms, curricula, assessment, educational technology, language policy, multilingualism, second-language learning, discourse, identity, literacy, institutions, organisations, communities, policy, communication, culture, inequality, attitudes, behaviour, or social relationships.

For education, applied linguistics, language education, and TESOL:

- Do not cycle among *student*, *learner*, *participant*, *pupil*, and *individual* unless the referent changes.
- Distinguish teachers, instructors, tutors, facilitators, and teacher educators when the study does.
- Keep constructs stable, including willingness to communicate, learner autonomy, teacher cognition, language proficiency, engagement, self-efficacy, translanguaging, corrective feedback, and academic literacy.
- Do not treat EFL, ESL, EAL, L2, foreign language, additional language, and multilingual education as interchangeable.
- Retain local institutional and sociolinguistic context when it affects interpretation.
- Avoid deficit framing of multilingual learners, minoritised students, non-native English-speaking teachers, and under-resourced schools.
- Avoid judging learner language against an unexplained native-speaker norm.

For general social-science manuscripts:

- Preserve the field's constructs instead of replacing them with generic synonyms.
- Distinguish individuals, participants, households, organisations, institutions, communities, and populations as units of analysis.
- Keep structure, agency, identity, discourse, practice, power, culture, and context conceptually distinct when the argument does.
- Avoid psychological, cultural, biological, or technological determinism unless evidence and theory justify it.
- Do not turn statistical regularities into universal accounts of human behaviour.
- Preserve reflexivity, positionality, historical context, and power relations when methodologically relevant.

## Bias-free and REC wording

Read `../references/reporting/reporting-router.md`, then follow `../references/reporting/jars-rec.md` for race, ethnicity, culture, positionality, population context, and limits on generality. Prefer the community's or study's chosen labels. Do not silently modernise identity terms when that would alter source meaning.

## Section calibration

### Introduction and literature review

- Move efficiently from problem or debate to gap, purpose, and contribution.
- Synthesise studies by claim, tension, method, context, or theoretical position rather than listing one source per sentence.
- Replace rhetorical gaps such as *few studies have examined* only when the literature supports a more precise gap.
- Do not inflate novelty with *groundbreaking*, *pioneering*, *unprecedented*, or *first ever* unless verified and necessary.
- Explain what a theoretical framework enables the study to examine; do not use theory as decorative authority.

### Method

- Preserve exact design, participants, setting, instruments, procedures, analytic decisions, and ethics information.
- Use stable labels for groups, measures, datasets, and analytic stages.
- Do not convert methodological limitations into vague strengths or vice versa.
- For qualitative work, preserve reflexivity and researcher involvement. Do not write that themes simply *emerged* when the method treats analysis as interpretive.
- For interventions, distinguish implementation, participation, exposure, fidelity, and outcomes.

### Results and findings

- Keep values, statistical direction, uncertainty, table references, theme names, and evidentiary extracts unchanged unless correcting a verified error.
- Separate reporting from interpretation according to the journal's convention.
- Do not translate nonsignificance into *no effect* or association into causation.
- Describe the substantive pattern, magnitude, shape, or patterned meaning before relying on significance labels; do not add nonlinearities, thresholds, subgroup differences, or anomalies absent from the verified output.
- Do not smooth participant speech into standard English unless the declared transcription policy permits it.

### Discussion and conclusion

- State the finding before explaining it.
- Link interpretation to evidence, literature, theory, and context.
- Keep alternative explanations and boundary conditions visible.
- Keep claim-specific qualifications beside the relevant interpretation. Consolidate study-wide design, sample, measurement, and transfer limitations in a dedicated limitations passage, then avoid repeating them mechanically after every finding.
- Calibrate implications to the design, sample, setting, and duration. A small, local, observational, or short-term study rarely supports universal prescriptions.
- Distinguish implications for teaching, teacher education, curriculum, assessment, policy, theory, and future research.
- End with bounded significance or contribution, not a slogan or generic call for more research.

## Formulaic-language diagnostics

Treat these as editing heuristics, not proof that prose was generated by AI.

## Optional house-style recommendations from reviewer experience

Use these recommendations when they improve the manuscript's precision or reviewer readability. They are flexible preferences, not mandatory quotas, word bans, or a replacement for evidence, reporting standards, or the author's judgement.

### Calibrate wording to evidence

- Reserve *significantly* for statistical significance supported by the reported test and *p* value. For descriptive magnitude, use *markedly* or *substantially* only when the evidence supports that description.
- Match verbs to the evidence: use *indicate*, *show*, or *demonstrate* when the data directly support the bounded claim; use *suggest* for a reasonable inference that is not directly established; use *may* or *might* for a genuine possibility or unresolved explanation; use *likely* or *potentially* only when the qualification has a defensible basis.
- Use *speculate* sparingly. When repeated speculation makes the Discussion sound impressionistic, state a precise possible explanation with *may*/*might* or *suggest*, and connect it to relevant evidence or literature.
- Retain *interestingly* only when the sentence explains what is unexpected or why the pattern matters. Otherwise state the finding directly or remove the adverb.
- Use *however* when a real contrast or concession is being made. Vary or remove transitions when repetition makes the prose mechanical; do not impose a fixed per-paragraph limit.
- Reserve *novel* for a contribution supported by a sufficiently verified literature search. When the stronger claim is uncertain, prefer *previously unreported*, *not fully explored*, *extends*, or another more precise formulation.

### Preserve section-level coherence

- When revising a complete article, keep the Discussion opening focused on the central findings and contribution rather than repeating the Introduction's background.
- When a null or unsupported result is central, give it enough interpretation to explain what the data do and do not establish, including relevant precision, design, or measurement limits. Do not translate nonsignificance into proof of no effect.
- Present a small number of consequential limitations when appropriate. Explain the consequence of each and, where feasible, the kind of evidence or design that could address it. A limitation should bound the conclusion rather than overturn it rhetorically unless it genuinely undermines the claim.
- Write figure captions so readers can understand the figure without the main text: identify what it shows, the data or sample, units, panels, abbreviations, and relevant analysis or uncertainty information. Add only details supported by the manuscript and output.

These recommendations are prompts for editorial judgement. Skip them when they do not fit the article, design, journal, or user's request; do not turn them into mechanical self-check counts.

### Inflation and promotion

Review *pivotal*, *crucial*, *transformative*, *profound*, *groundbreaking*, *landmark*, *rapidly evolving landscape*, *underscores the importance*, *holds immense potential*, *revolutionises learning*, and *unlocks potential*. Replace evaluation with the specific problem, evidence, or consequence.

Before:
> Artificial intelligence represents a transformative force in the rapidly evolving landscape of language education, underscoring the crucial need to prepare teachers for an unprecedented future.

After:
> Generative AI is increasingly used in language-learning tasks, but teachers remain uncertain about how to evaluate its pedagogical value and risks.

### Superficial participial endings

Review clauses beginning with *highlighting*, *underscoring*, *showcasing*, *demonstrating*, *fostering*, or *contributing to* when they merely add praise.

Before:
> The intervention group reported greater willingness to communicate, highlighting the transformative role of collaborative tasks.

After:
> The intervention group reported greater willingness to communicate. This difference may be related to the repeated use of collaborative tasks, although the study did not test that mechanism directly.

### Vague attribution

Keep *previous studies suggest* when followed by appropriate citations. Replace unsupported *experts agree*, *research has proven*, and *it is widely believed* with attributable, bounded claims. If evidence is missing, flag the claim rather than inserting a placeholder citation.

### Source-by-source literature dumping

Combine studies around an argument rather than varying only the reporting verb.

Before:
> Smith (2021) examined feedback. Lee (2022) investigated feedback. Chen (2023) explored feedback in online classes.

After:
> Research on written feedback has examined its form, learner uptake, and delivery mode, but findings differ across face-to-face and online settings (Smith, 2021; Lee, 2022; Chen, 2023).

### Abstract vocabulary clusters

Review clusters of *delve*, *multifaceted*, *intricate interplay*, *holistic*, *dynamic landscape*, *valuable insights*, *robust understanding*, and *nuanced tapestry*. Do not blacklist words in isolation. Keep them when they are the clearest technical choice.

Prefer concrete verbs:

- *delves into teacher beliefs* -> *examines teacher beliefs*
- *provides valuable insights into participation* -> *shows how participation differed across tasks*
- *serves as a barrier to participation* -> *limits participation*
- *conducted an examination of* -> *examined*

Retain nominalisations that are established constructs, such as *language socialisation* or *investment*.

### Corrective "not X but Y" framing

Avoid presenting ordinary claims as a staged correction. This speech-like pattern often creates artificial drama and can obscure the actual relation between concepts.

Watch for:

- *not X but Y*
- *not merely X but Y*
- *not just X; it is Y*
- *not only X but also Y*
- *more than X, Y*

Before:
> Teacher identity is not a fixed attribute but a dynamic process shaped by institutional expectations.

After:
> Teacher identity develops through teachers' engagement with institutional expectations.

Before:
> Translanguaging is not merely a classroom strategy; it is a transformative orientation towards multilingualism.

After:
> Translanguaging can function as a classroom strategy and as an orientation towards multilingualism, depending on how the term is conceptualised.

Keep a negative-positive contrast only when the rejected interpretation is substantively present in the literature or data. Even then, name that interpretation and explain why it is inadequate instead of relying on the formula alone.

### Paired em-dash clause insertion

Avoid sentences that interrupt the main clause with explanatory text enclosed by two em dashes. This pattern often makes prose sound scripted, especially when repeated across paragraphs.

Before:
> Teacher feedback — particularly feedback delivered during peer interaction — shaped how learners revised their texts.

Weak repair:
> Teacher feedback, particularly feedback delivered during peer interaction, shaped how learners revised their texts.

The weak repair changes only punctuation and preserves the same interruptive structure.

Better repair:
> Feedback delivered during peer interaction shaped how learners revised their texts.

Alternative repair when both categories matter:
> Teacher feedback shaped how learners revised their texts. Feedback delivered during peer interaction was especially influential.

Choose among integration, sentence division, a relative clause, a colon, or a semicolon according to the logical relationship. Preserve the inserted information only when it contributes to the claim.

### Synonym cycling

Repeat technical terms intentionally. Do not rotate *learners*, *students*, *participants*, and *respondents* for variety. Do not rotate *beliefs*, *perceptions*, *attitudes*, and *views* if the study treats them as different constructs.

### Stacked hedging

Use one well-chosen layer of caution rather than several.

Before:
> These findings may possibly suggest that collaborative dialogue could potentially have a beneficial influence on learner engagement.

After:
> These findings suggest that collaborative dialogue may support learner engagement.

Do not remove necessary caution from qualitative interpretation, observational findings, exploratory analyses, or small samples.

Do not blacklist phrases such as *further research is needed*, *may not be generalisable*, or *should be interpreted with caution*. Remove them when they are generic, repetitive, or detached from a named limitation. Replace them with the specific unresolved question, population boundary, assumption, or source of uncertainty.

### Causal and prescriptive overclaiming

Match verbs to design:

- randomised or strongly identified effect: *increased*, *reduced*, or *led to*, when justified
- observational association: *was associated with* or *corresponded with*
- qualitative interpretation: *participants described*, *the analysis suggests*, or *accounts constructed*
- recommendation: *may inform* or *suggests a need to consider*, not automatically *teachers should* or *policymakers must*

Before:
> The interviews prove that translanguaging improves achievement and should be adopted in all language classrooms.

After:
> Participants described translanguaging as helping them clarify difficult content. The finding supports further examination of when such practices assist learning across different classroom contexts.

### Mechanical transitions

Keep *however*, *therefore*, *in addition*, *by contrast*, *similarly*, and *taken together* when the relationship is real. Remove or vary them only when repeated mechanically. Never delete a transition and leave disconnected sentences.

### Repetition and empty evaluation

Remove adjacent paraphrases introduced by *in other words*, *put differently*, or *essentially* when they add no information. Replace *this is an important finding* with the specific reason it matters.

Before:
> This is an important finding with significant pedagogical implications.

After:
> The finding indicates that task instructions may shape whether quieter learners participate in group discussion.

### Citation and quotation integrity

- Do not remove citations merely to make prose look less dense.
- Do not add citations to claims the source may not support.
- Do not rewrite direct quotations as authorial prose.
- Preserve participant meaning and the study's transcription conventions.

## Toulmin check

For contested claims, test claim, data, warrant, backing, qualifier, and rebuttal. Add only the components needed to make the reasoning visible; do not force every sentence into a template.

## Editing workflow

### Pass 1: Protect the research

1. Identify the article section, design, central claim, evidence, and required terminology.
2. Mark content that must not change: data, quotations, citations, theme names, hypotheses, RQs, instrument names, and statistical direction.
3. Infer the author's voice from nearby prose.

### Pass 2: Improve language dynamics

4. Repair paragraph purpose, old-to-new flow, sentence links, and rhythm.
5. Remove inflation, promotional wording, vague attribution, repetition, and empty evaluation.
6. Localise qualifications: retain claim-specific boundaries beside the claim and move repeated study-level caveats to the limitations passage. When editing an isolated excerpt and that destination is unavailable, retain one specific study-level qualification and flag it for later consolidation.
7. Tighten hedging without increasing certainty.
8. Restore term consistency and disciplinary precision.
9. Calibrate claims and implications to the method, sample, educational or social context, and target population.
10. Check rhetorical moves against the Manchester Phrasebank, Discussion guide, and any user-supplied exemplar blueprint.

### Final audit

11. Confirm that no fact, number, citation, quotation, construct, or analytic conclusion changed unintentionally.
12. Search for paired em-dash clause insertions. Rebuild the sentence rather than swapping punctuation marks; review any remaining single em dash against the journal and author's established style.
13. Check terminology for learners, teachers, languages, settings, social groups, and units of analysis.
14. Check every paragraph for claim-evidence-explanation-qualification flow.
15. Audit caveat placement: each qualifier must name what is limited and why; generic study-level caveats should not recur across paragraphs.
16. When an exemplar was used, confirm that the revision borrowed only abstract rhetorical moves, not distinctive wording, claims, citations, or sentence-level imitation.
17. Read the revision aloud for stiffness, repetition, abrupt transitions, and unnatural cadence.

## Output

Provide the revised text and a short note covering substantive language decisions, preserved uncertainties, and claims or citations requiring author verification. If the user requests clean text only, return only the revision.

## Genre calibration

Default to compressed journal-article prose with visible contribution and tight paragraph economy. Use `../references/genres/academic-genres.md` only when the user is clearly writing another genre.
