# APRIL — JARS–Qual

Adapted from the JARS–Qual primary qualitative table and the qualitative meta-analysis table currently present in the vault source PDFs:

- `JARS_qual-table-1.pdf` — primary qualitative research
- `JARS_qual-table-2.pdf` — qualitative meta-analysis / QMARS

Use the primary qualitative checklist for original qualitative studies. Use the QMARS extension when the manuscript synthesizes prior qualitative studies instead of collecting new field data.

## Primary qualitative research (Table 1)

### Title page and abstract
- Title: identify the key issue or topic clearly.
- Author note: acknowledge funding, contributors, and conflicts.
- Abstract: state the problem or question, design, participants or data sources, analytic strategy, main findings, implications, and **five keywords**.
- If useful and legible within the word limit, name the approach to inquiry in the abstract.

### Introduction
- Frame the problem or question and its context.
- Review, critique, and synthesize the relevant literature, including debates, frameworks, barriers, gaps, and practical needs.
- State study objectives, aims, or goals.
- Name the target audience if relevant.
- Give the rationale for fit between the design and the goal.
- Describe the approach to inquiry when it clarifies the rationale.
- If relevant, explain how the analysis relates to prior publications from the same project.
- Prefer research questions and goals over NHST-style hypotheses unless the tradition genuinely uses hypotheses.

### Method

#### Research design overview
- Summarize data-collection and analytic strategies.
- Describe the approach to inquiry if it helps readers understand the design.
- Provide the rationale for the selected design.
- Accept that qualitative methods may be iterative rather than linear; collection and analysis can be combined when the design requires it.

#### Researcher description
- Describe the researchers' backgrounds and prior understandings of the phenomenon.
- Explain how those understandings were managed and how they shaped collection or analysis.
- Include credentials, experience, values, demographic or cultural positioning, or archival decisions when relevant.

#### Participants or other data sources
- Give the number of participants, documents, or events analyzed.
- Describe demographics, cultural information, perspectives, or other characteristics that may shape the data.
- Describe existing or archival data sources when used.
- Provide repository information if openly shared.
- Describe how archival material or source texts were located.

#### Researcher-participant relationship
- Describe prior relationships and interactions relevant to the study.
- Note ethical implications and any effect on the research process.

#### Recruitment and selection
- Describe the recruitment process, protocols, incentives, consent and ethics procedures, and how sample size was determined.
- Report attrition, refusals, and reasons where relevant.
- State the rationale for stopping data collection, such as saturation or design-based sufficiency.
- Describe the selection strategy: purposive, convenience, snowball, theoretical, maximum-variation, and so on.
- Provide inclusion and exclusion criteria and study context such as site and timing.

#### Data collection and recording
- State the form of data collected: interviews, observations, questionnaires, media, archives, and so on.
- Describe how the protocol originated or evolved.
- Report changes in collection strategy made in response to emerging findings or design logic.
- Describe the collection context, duration, frequency, and degree of engagement.
- For interview-based work, report mean and range of duration where useful.
- Describe the management of reflexivity during collection.
- Clarify the content and form of questions rather than dumping full interview guides.
- Identify recording, field note, transcription, and data transformation procedures.

#### Data analysis
- Describe the analytic strategy and purpose in accessible terms.
- Explain the analytic process in detail, including coding, thematization, interpretation, unitization, or related procedures.
- Identify coders or analysts and their training.
- State whether coding categories emerged from the analysis or were defined a priori.
- Identify units of analysis and how those units were formed.
- Describe how the analytic scheme was developed and refined.
- Name software if used.

#### Methodological integrity
- Show that the claims are warranted and methodologically sound.
- Address data adequacy in relation to diversity, question, and approach.
- Show how researcher perspective was managed in both collection and analysis.
- Demonstrate grounding in evidence through quotations, excerpts, or comparable material.
- Show that contributions are insightful and meaningful relative to the study goal and literature.
- Provide contextual information needed to interpret the findings.
- Present findings coherently, including contradictions or disconfirming evidence.
- Describe consistency procedures or how inconsistency was handled.
- If additional checks were used, name them specifically: member feedback, triangulation, audit, consensus, matrices, reflexive journaling, thick description, and so on.
- Do not invent empty rigor claims. If a check was not used, omit it.

### Findings / Results
- Report themes, categories, narratives, or meanings in a form that fits the design.
- Use excerpts as evidence, not as a substitute for analysis.
- Show the logic of the analysis where needed.
- Use synthesizing displays or diagrams when they help.
- Quantification is optional and should appear only when it serves the qualitative goal.

### Discussion
- State the central contributions and significance.
- Explain how the findings support, extend, complicate, or challenge prior work.
- Consider alternative explanations or interpretations.
- Describe strengths and limitations in relation to data quality, sources, procedures, and methodological integrity.
- Discuss **transferability** rather than statistical generalizability.
- Note ethical dilemmas encountered when relevant.
- Identify implications for research, policy, or practice.
- Allow for more than one valid interpretation where the tradition permits it.

## Qualitative meta-analysis / QMARS (Table 2)
Use when the manuscript synthesizes prior qualitative studies rather than reporting a new primary qualitative dataset.

### Core additions
- State that the work is a qualitative meta-analysis and name the form: metasynthesis, meta-ethnography, critical interpretive synthesis, narrative synthesis, and so on.
- Describe the problem, the body of literature to be synthesized, and the relevant debates or frameworks.
- State the meta-analytic goals and the intended contribution.
- Describe the study-selection process in detail:
  - search strategy
  - inclusion and exclusion criteria
  - databases and other sources searched
  - final number of studies and how that number was reached
- Summarize the reviewed studies, ideally in a table:
  - publication year
  - discipline
  - geography
  - language
  - data-collection method
  - analytic method
  - study purpose
  - participant count
  - recruitment method
- Describe how findings were extracted from primary studies, including:
  - what parts of each study were used
  - coding units
  - coding process
  - team discussion and consensus process
  - software if used
  - whether categories were emergent or predefined
- Comment on the integrity of both the meta-analysis and the underlying primary studies.
- In findings, move beyond summary:
  - synthesize themes and meanings
  - use quotations from primary studies when helpful
  - examine contradictions and contextual differences across studies
  - present diagrams, tables, or models when they improve clarity
- In discussion, interpret beyond recap:
  - explain contribution to the literature
  - link back to disputes or frameworks
  - state transferability limits
  - discuss implications for research, policy, or practice
