# APRIL - Confident qualification and exemplar learning

Use this guide when a manuscript sounds defensive, repeats generic caution, disperses the same limitation across sections, or must learn reasoning and assertion from a strong exemplar without copying its prose.

## Core rule

Write the strongest claim warranted by the evidence, then qualify the exact part that remains uncertain. Confidence comes from a visible claim-evidence-warrant chain, not from removing necessary limits.

Do not put every caveat in the Limitations section. Some qualifications determine whether the sentence is accurate and must remain beside the claim.

## Place qualifications by function

| Qualification type | Default placement | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Estimate uncertainty, exploratory status, model dependence, or noncausal design | Beside the result or interpretation | State once, specifically, where it changes the claim |
| Boundary condition for a theory or mechanism | In the same paragraph as the interpretation | Name the population, setting, process, or assumption |
| Study-wide sampling, measurement, duration, missingness, or design limitation | Dedicated limitations passage | Explain consequence and scope once; avoid mechanical repetition |
| Future research | End of Discussion or limitations/future-work passage | Name the unresolved question and suitable next design |
| Routine methodological detail already reported | Method or Results | Do not repeat as defensive prose in Discussion |

If moving a qualifier would make the original claim misleading, keep it local. If the qualifier applies to the whole study and has already been explained, consolidate it.

When editing an isolated excerpt without access to its eventual Limitations passage, retain a necessary study-wide qualification once and flag it for later consolidation. Do not delete the only available statement of an important boundary.

## Separate self-protection from substantive qualification

Assess a disclaimer-like sentence or clause by what it does. Remove apologies, claims of effort, generic liability language, or pre-emptive defences whose function is to protect the author, model, or process rather than inform the reader. Retain genuine uncertainty, assumptions, alternative explanations, reproducibility or access limits, concrete risks, and required disclosures when they bound interpretation or guide action.

For mixed sentences, remove only the self-protective clause and make the smallest grammatical adjustment needed. Preserve the substantive claim, its meaning and evidential strength, the scope of each citation, any conditions, and commitments. Do not convert this edit into a general weakening of the claim or delete a real boundary. When function is unclear, retain the wording and flag it for author review. Use the normal brief author-facing edit note when the requested output permits one, or an existing editorial record only when authorised; do not create a separate governance file automatically. If the user requests clean text only, return clean text only.

## Build a confident paragraph

Begin every substantive paragraph with a claim-oriented topic sentence. State the bounded finding, argument, distinction or rationale that the rest of the paragraph develops. A topic label or an announcement of what the paragraph covers is insufficient.

Develop that claim through explanation, then evidence and examples that substantiate it. Make the link between the evidence and the claim explicit. Add a critical comment or a specific qualification when a tension, alternative explanation or boundary changes the interpretation. Close with a supported implication or connection when needed.

These functions may span several sentences and interact; they do not prescribe a fixed sentence count. Vary paragraph lengths with the complexity of the claim and support it needs. Keep section-appropriate procedural or descriptive openings in Method and Results, without inventing interpretive claims.

Do not append an automatic caution sentence to every paragraph. Do not retract a supported claim immediately after stating it. Remove self-protective openings and endings while preserving qualifications essential to the claim's accuracy.

## Repair generic caveats

Treat the following as diagnostic prompts, not forbidden phrases.

| Generic wording | Better move |
|---|---|
| “Further research is needed.” | Name what remains unknown, why the current design cannot resolve it, and which evidence would resolve it. |
| “The findings may not be generalisable.” | Name the sampled population and the specific population, setting, language, institution, or period to which transfer remains uncertain. |
| “The result applies under limited conditions.” | State the conditions and explain how they bound the claim. |
| “This result should be interpreted with caution.” | Name the source of uncertainty, such as imprecision, measurement, selection, model dependence, multiplicity, or exploratory status. |

Example:

Before:
> This result should be interpreted with caution and may not be generalisable. Further research is needed.

After:
> The association was estimated from self-reports collected at two urban universities. Its transfer to rural secondary-school settings remains untested.

The revised wording is firmer because it replaces ritual caution with an explicit boundary.

## Use an assertion ladder

Choose the strongest rung the design supports:

- **Documented result:** “The analysis identified …” or “Participants described …”
- **Bounded interpretation:** “This pattern suggests …”
- **Observed relation:** “X was associated with Y …”
- **Causal effect:** “X increased Y …” only when the design and identification support it
- **Proposed explanation:** “One possible explanation is …” when the mechanism was not tested

Do not use weaker language merely to sound cautious, and do not move to a stronger rung for rhetorical force.

## Learn from an exemplar without copying

When the user supplies a strong paper, analyse its rhetorical architecture rather than its wording.

1. Identify the section and communicative purpose.
2. Map paragraph functions: claim, evidence, warrant, literature relation, qualification, implication, and closure.
3. Record where the author places the main assertion, how evidence accumulates, where qualifications appear, and how paragraphs transition.
4. Convert the pattern into an abstract blueprint using placeholders for the user's claims and evidence.
5. Draft from the user's manuscript, citations, data, and established voice; do not draft by substituting synonyms into the exemplar.
6. Compare only at the level of reasoning sequence, information flow, stance, paragraph length, and move distribution.

Do not copy or closely paraphrase distinctive phrases, metaphors, claims, citations, examples, or sentence structures. Do not imitate an author's idiosyncratic voice. Preserve the target journal's conventions and the manuscript author's own terminology.

## Final audit

- Does each substantive paragraph begin with a supported claim-oriented topic sentence, then develop that claim through explanation, evidence, examples and any warranted critical comment?
- Do paragraph lengths vary with the argument, without equal-length blocks, padding or fragmented support?
- Does every qualifier identify what is uncertain and why?
- Are study-wide limitations explained once rather than scattered?
- Have generic caution phrases been replaced with specific boundaries where possible?
- Does the conclusion retain warranted confidence without exceeding the design?
- If an exemplar was used, can every sentence be traced to the user's evidence and argument rather than the exemplar's language?
