# APRIL optional empirical storytelling

Use this optional guide only when the user asks for stronger storytelling, narrative depth, or argument coherence, or when an authorised drafting/review task would clearly benefit from it. Here, *story* means a coherent question–evidence–answer sequence. It does not mean dramatisation, selective reporting, decorative anecdotes, or suspense.

This is a rhetorical enhancement resource, not a method, reporting standard, JARS requirement, or condition of manuscript validity. A rigorous article need not contain a puzzle, mechanism, boundary condition, or surprising result. Skip any move that does not fit the study, evidence, genre, or user's request; do not report its absence as a defect unless narrative development is explicitly being assessed.

## Core principle

When useful, narrative depth can come from explanatory progression:

1. **Phenomenon:** What pattern, contrast, process, experience, or outcome requires explanation?
2. **Mechanism or interpretation:** Through what measured process, participant account, theoretical logic, or causal pathway might it occur?
3. **Condition or boundary:** When, where, for whom, or under what circumstances does the pattern strengthen, weaken, change form, or fail?

Use only the deepest layer the design and evidence support. A descriptive study may establish the phenomenon without identifying a mechanism. An observational association may motivate an explanation without testing it. A moderator, subgroup, threshold, context, or deviant case becomes a boundary condition only when the analysis and evidence justify that interpretation.

Do not force every article into all three layers. Label an untested mechanism as a proposed explanation and an untested condition as a future or theoretical proposition.

## Article-level spine

When the manuscript benefits from an explicit story spine, build one traceable progression:

**evidence-grounded phenomenon or puzzle → existing explanation → unresolved tension → study question and design → ordered evidence → bounded answer → contribution**

- A **puzzle** is a verified mismatch between expectation and observation, conflicting credible accounts, or a consequential pattern that current explanations do not resolve. It is not a rhetorical question invented to create drama.
- The study should promise only the distinction its evidence can adjudicate. Do not imply that the analysis tests a mechanism, condition, or competing theory unless it does.
- Maintain the same central question across sections. Each section performs a different job rather than restarting the paper with a new rationale.

## Section-specific moves

### Ideation and research questions

- Begin from a consequential phenomenon or tension, not merely a broad topic.
- Separate what is observed, what is unexplained, candidate explanations, and possible boundary conditions.
- Formulate RQs or hypotheses that discriminate among plausible accounts. Do not add mechanism or moderation hypotheses merely to make the story appear deeper.
- Record the intended story layer and what evidence would be required to support it.

### Introduction

- Open with a concrete, evidenced phenomenon, contradiction, or practical/theoretical difficulty when one exists.
- Convert it into an answerable scholarly question and show why the current literature cannot yet answer it adequately.
- Use an evidence-based gap as part of the reasoning. Avoid a generic “X has been studied, but Y has been ignored” sentence when it does not reveal the underlying problem.
- Preview the perspective, design, or comparison through which the study will resolve or refine the question.

### Literature review

Organise the literature as a scholarly dialogue:

1. establish the leading account or recurring finding;
2. identify its scope, unresolved evidence, or competing interpretation;
3. introduce a complementary or conflicting account;
4. explain how the accounts relate and where their predictions differ;
5. derive the study's model, question, or hypothesis and any theoretically justified condition.

Do not manufacture a binary debate when the literature is cumulative or complementary. Every account and disagreement must be supported by verified sources.

### Method

- Explain why the design, sample, measures, timing, comparisons, and analytic strategy can answer the RQ or distinguish the proposed accounts.
- Connect each consequential choice to an estimand, interpretive objective, validity threat, or boundary of inference.
- Report how the study was conducted, but also state the design logic where it affects what the findings can mean.
- Do not retrofit a theoretical rationale to an analysis selected after seeing the results. Label exploratory decisions transparently.

### Results or Findings

Order evidence by RQ, hypothesis, or analytic claim rather than by the order in which commands were run. For each major finding:

1. identify the comparison, model, theme, or evidentiary basis;
2. describe the direction, magnitude, shape, variation, or patterned meaning, with uncertainty or evidentiary support;
3. point to the relevant table, figure, extract, or diagnostic;
4. state the bounded empirical answer without importing a full theoretical explanation.

Translate estimates into substantive meaning when the scale permits. Describe a nonlinearity, threshold, subgroup pattern, sequence, or anomaly only when it was actually estimated or evidenced. Do not turn a p value into a story, a table into prose line by line, or an association into a mechanism.

### Discussion

- Lead with the answer earned by the findings, then explain why it matters.
- Relate the result to the literature as confirmation, refinement, extension, qualification, or challenge.
- Distinguish a tested mechanism from a theory-consistent interpretation and from an alternative explanation.
- Identify evidenced boundary conditions and explain how they change the scope of the claim.
- Discuss an unexpected, contradictory, null, or deviant pattern when it genuinely changes the interpretation. Do not invent a surprise as a compulsory rhetorical move.
- End the interpretive sequence with the contribution and its limits, rather than with generic praise or an unbounded recommendation.

### Conclusion and abstract

- The conclusion should close the question opened in the Introduction and state the deeper answer now supported, without adding evidence.
- The abstract should preserve the compact spine: phenomenon/problem → question/aim → design → principal empirical pattern → bounded implication or contribution.

## Evidence-status labels

Keep these distinctions visible in notes and prose:

| Status | Permitted treatment |
|---|---|
| Observed pattern | Describe from verified data, output, extracts, or cases |
| Tested mechanism | Interpret only from a design and analysis that identify or directly examine the process |
| Proposed explanation | Present as theory-consistent or plausible, with alternatives |
| Tested boundary condition | Report the relevant interaction, subgroup contrast, context comparison, temporal pattern, or qualitative variation |
| Proposed boundary | Frame as a limitation, proposition, or future test |
| Unexpected result | Call unexpected only relative to a documented hypothesis, prediction, or well-supported expectation |

## Visual storytelling

Use a figure when it reveals the central empirical pattern more clearly than prose or a table. Match the visual to the evidence: effect/uncertainty plot, marginal-effect curve, event-time plot, profile, joint display, thematic map, sequence diagram, or case comparison. Never add a curve, threshold, mechanism diagram, or subgroup split that the analysis does not support.

## Optional narrative audit

Use this audit only within an authorised storytelling, structural-editing, or narrative-review task. An unmet item is not automatically a methodological or reporting defect.

1. Can a reader state the paper's central empirical question in one sentence?
2. Does the Introduction establish a real phenomenon or puzzle rather than only announce a topic or absence?
3. Does the literature review show relationships among accounts instead of displaying sources sequentially?
4. Does the Method explain why consequential choices make the intended inference possible?
5. Does each Results subsection describe a substantive pattern, magnitude, or meaning rather than only significance or table contents?
6. Does the Discussion explain the finding as far as the evidence permits, including an actual complication or boundary when one exists?
7. Are mechanisms, conditions, surprises, and causal claims labelled according to their evidence status?
8. Do the Introduction, Results, Discussion, Conclusion, and Abstract tell the same bounded research story without adding unrequested sections or evidence?
