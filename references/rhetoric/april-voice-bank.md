## Preferred vocabulary

### spectre

April's preference: use more. Confirmed 20261107; supplied as "spectre".

Usage guidance: the British English spelling; in its figurative sense, a feared possibility or threatening prospect. Useful forms include *the spectre of* and *raise the spectre of*. Use when apprehension or threat is part of the intended meaning. It can intensify a claim, so do not add it to a neutral description of uncertainty or imply that a feared outcome has occurred.

Illustrative wording, not a research finding: "The spectre of job displacement features in debates about educational automation."

### vis-à-vis

April's preference: use more. Confirmed 20261107; supplied as "vis-A-vis", normalised here to "vis-à-vis".

Usage guidance: expresses a relation or comparison, as in *in relation to* or *compared with*. Use when positioning one concept, group or role in relation to another. Make the intended relationship clear in context; the expression alone does not specify its direction or establish a difference. Use ordinary roman type by default unless the journal requires otherwise.

Illustrative wording, not a research finding: "The study examines teachers' professional authority vis-à-vis automated assessment systems."

### contrariwise

April's preference: use more. Confirmed 20261107; supplied as "Contrariwise". Capitalise at the start of a sentence; use lowercase elsewhere.

Usage guidance: introduces an opposing proposition or the reverse relationship. Use when the preceding argument establishes a clear point of contrast and the formal register fits. It is stronger than a general transition such as *however*; preserve the actual logical relationship.

## Expressions to use less

### rather than

April's preference: use less. Confirmed 20261107.

Review habitual corrective contrasts and state the intended focus, action or relationship directly when the rejected alternative adds nothing. Retain a substantive distinction when removing it would change the argument; rebuild the sentence if another structure expresses it more naturally. Preserve exact quotations. Do not replace every occurrence mechanically with *instead of* or another repeated contrast.

Illustrative revision when the alternative has no argumentative role:

- Before: "The analysis focuses on teachers' interpretations rather than offering a comprehensive evaluation of the platform."
- After: "The analysis focuses on teachers' interpretations."

Keep the scope distinction explicit if the surrounding argument depends on it.

### plausible

April's preference: use less. Confirmed 20261107.

State the specific explanation, its evidential basis and any unresolved inference when that is clearer. Preserve warranted uncertainty; removing *plausible* must not make a possible explanation sound established. Retain exact quotations and necessary technical uses. Do not substitute another vague hedge mechanically.

### boundary

April's preference: use less, including unnecessary plural uses of *boundaries*. Confirmed 20261107.

Name the intended meaning precisely: *scope*, *limits*, *conditions*, *distinction* or another expression only when it accurately describes the argument. Preserve established constructs, necessary technical uses such as *boundary conditions* or *boundary crossing*, and exact quotations. Avoid mechanical substitution or changing a construct to satisfy this preference.

## Preferred sentence patterns

### Conceptual confusion located in its evidential context

April explicitly selected this pattern on 20261107. User-supplied exemplar; source not identified:

> Inconsistency or confusion with respect to this distinction between
> construct domain and task universe is apparent historically, especially
> in relation to the form of evidence offered to support relevance and
> representativeness.

Learn its argumentative progression: identify a conceptual difficulty; name the precise distinction; situate the difficulty historically or within a defined body of work; then specify the evidence or methodological practice through which it becomes apparent. The sentence advances a diagnostic claim and narrows its scope in the same movement.

Transfer this progression when an argument needs it. Use a precise distinction and a concrete evidential focus, with the qualification attached to the aspect it limits. Choose one sentence or connected sentences according to readability; preserve the user's preference for varied sentence length. Historical generalisations and claims of inconsistency require support in the manuscript.

Illustrative adaptation with no empirical claim:

> Earlier work on [topic] sometimes conflated [X] and [Y], a difficulty most evident in [specific evidence or methodological practice].

Borrow the rhetorical movement without closely reproducing distinctive wording or transferring the exemplar's substantive claims. This example does not establish its individual phrases as preferred vocabulary or require every paragraph to diagnose confusion.
