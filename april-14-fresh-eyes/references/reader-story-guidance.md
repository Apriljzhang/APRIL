# First-reader research narrative guidance

Use this reference for Fresh Eyes' final post-revision reading, not to expand it into peer review or substantive rewriting. A good research story here means an intelligible, evidence-faithful account of what was investigated, why, how, what was found or developed, and what it means. The reader should be able to follow the relationships, rather than infer the author's drafting intentions.

## Sources and the decisions they inform

- [Rice, White and Bircher, Yale Poorvu Center: Coherence and Flow](https://poorvucenter.yale.edu/sites/default/files/2024-12/Coherence%20and%20Flow%20From%20Old%20to%20New%20Information%20GWL%20Handout.pdf): distinguishes conceptual/organisational coherence from cohesion between textual units. Its reverse-outline exercise separates each paragraph's idea from its role in the larger account, and allows thematic, chronological and comparative structures. Use these as teaching heuristics, not a validated scoring instrument or a compulsory paragraph template.
- [Mensh and Kording (2017), Ten simple rules for structuring papers](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005619): emphasises the reader's unfamiliarity, links between context, contribution and meaning, stable terminology and pacing without confusing backtracking. It is an editorial oriented towards scientific IMRaD papers; do not universalise that format, a single central finding or its rhetorical prescriptions across all research traditions.
- [Australian National University, Flow](https://www.anu.edu.au/students/academic-skills/writing-assessment/editing-your-work/flow): illustrates helpful repetition of key concepts and summarising transitions, while warning about ambiguous demonstratives. Preserve repetition that helps the reader connect ideas. Examine the intended relationship itself; adding a connective cannot by itself supply missing reasoning.
- [Montagnes, Montagnes and Yang, Finding your scientific story by writing backwards](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s42995-021-00120-z) (published online 2021; journal volume 2022): treats story as a contextualised, coherent account beyond merely filling section headings; it also advises reading the finished manuscript forwards as a reader and warns against contrived post-hoc questions. Fresh Eyes adopts that forward-reading perspective, not its backward-writing sequence or a mandatory punchline.

## Operational questions for this skill

1. What does the opening lead a new reader to expect, and what does the finished paper actually deliver? If a promised thread disappears, ask whether to clarify scope, close the thread or update the promise; do not demand a new study.
2. Can the reader trace purpose/questions through the approach to findings and interpretation, including thematic, iterative or mixed-methods structures? Flag an unexplained bridge, abandoned promise, premature detail, disconnected literature summary or confusing backtrack only when it obstructs understanding.
3. In a brief internal reverse outline, what is each paragraph's main idea and what job does it do for its section? For each sentence, what does the reader understand before it, and what information, explanation, evidence, qualification, connection, orientation or synthesis does it add here?
4. Does repeated wording develop the account, orient the reader or supply a necessary recap? Keep consistent technical terms, a useful conclusion summary before an implication, meaningful boundary conditions and necessary signposts. Question circular praise, generic significance claims without nearby substance, or neighbouring passages repeating one explanation without progression.
5. Does the current manuscript sustain one understandable account without suppressing discrepant findings, overstating certainty/novelty/causality or implying an investigation different from the one reported? Multiple findings or unresolved interpretations can form an honest story. No hypotheses, linear sequence, hero finding or complete resolution is mandatory.

These questions, the promise–delivery check, revision-residue checks and comments-only protocol are APRIL's tailored synthesis. They are not an empirically validated rubric, a reporting standard or evidence of methodological quality. Reconcile candidates against the complete current manuscript before commenting; an initial uncertainty may resolve naturally, need only a signpost, or be immaterial. Use the smallest helpful comment and leave author decisions to April.
