# Stimulated recall interviews: design, conduct and interpretation

Use for stimulated recall (SR/SRI; 刺激回忆访谈), video/screen-cued interviews, task-process retrospection, GenAI feedback engagement and gaze-cued recall. The term is **stimulated recall**, rather than simulated recall. Apply APRIL's operating and manuscript contracts, and produce only the protocol, critique, plan, Method prose or analysis support the user requests. Designing an interview does not authorise recording participants, processing their data or running an analysis.

## Define the evidence the study seeks

State whether the research question concerns participants' reported thoughts or feelings during a specific earlier event, their present-day interpretation of that event, or both. If both are needed, separate the recall and reflection phases in the instructions, interview record and analysis. Retrospective accounts are situated reports shaped by memory, cues, language and the interview interaction; they are not direct recordings of cognition. Do not silently convert a present-day explanation into an event-time mental state.

SR is an elicitation/data-generation procedure. Select the analytical approach independently from the question and epistemology. A structured codebook or content analysis can use `../../april-04-analysis/methods/qualitative-coding-analysis.md`; an explicitly chosen reflexive thematic analysis follows `../../april-04-analysis/methods/qualitative-rta.md` and its own quality criteria. A mixed design needs a justified integration point, rather than assuming that multiple data sources validate one another.

## Design the protocol

Document the original task, participants, language/proficiency, setting, cue type, event-sampling rule and intended claims. Explain how participants and episodes are selected, what is omitted and how that selection limits inference. Use task-linked video, audio, screen capture, screenshots, writing or chat logs that can be aligned with the original activity.

Record the actual task-to-interview interval and its variation, interview duration and any intervening tests, feedback, discussion or repeated exposure. For event-time recall, use the shortest feasible delay and explain practical compromises; no universal delay cutoff guarantees valid recollection. Distinguish methodological preference from what actually happened. Record the sequencing in both directions: tests or feedback before SR may change later accounts, while recall/replay before a post-test may influence subsequent learning or test performance. Do not assume the procedure is non-reactive because one study found no statistically significant group difference.

State who selects clips/stops, who controls playback and pausing, whether replay is allowed, how researcher-selected stops are explained and how the interviewer participates. Pilot the instructions, neutral practice and prompts with the intended population. When feasible, let participants use their preferred/strongest language. Report switching, interviewer proficiency, transcription and translation decisions, including how translated excerpts retain their link to the original.

For GenAI work, preserve the original prompts, displayed outputs, tool/model versions where known and timestamps. Regenerating an answer creates a different event and is unsuitable as an undisclosed replacement cue.

## Conduct the interview

Explain that there are no right answers, that “I do not remember” is acceptable and that uncertainty is useful evidence. Permit pausing/replay under the stated protocol. Avoid turning the interview into teaching, assessment, correction or a demand that the participant justify their behaviour.

Use a neutral prompt anchored to the earlier event, for example: “At this point, what, if anything, do you remember thinking?” Probe the participant's own wording rather than introducing the researcher's preferred categories, such as confusion, noticing, trust or cognitive load. Where temporal status is unclear, ask: “Was that something you remember from then, or something you are thinking now?” If present-day reflection is wanted, explicitly signal the phase change and retain it in the transcript.

Keep the actual instructions, prompts and consequential deviations. Describe interviewer relationships and power dynamics that might influence reports, particularly when a teacher interviews their own students. Do not treat fluent, detailed accounts as inherently more accurate than uncertain or sparse ones.

## Prepare and analyse the evidence

Maintain a traceable chain from original-event timestamp to cue/stop ID, interview turn and analytic claim. Preserve three distinct layers: what the recording/log shows, what the participant reports and what the researcher interprets. A useful episode record includes participant/task ID, event start/end, stimulus ID and selector, pause initiator, actual interviewer prompt, interview timestamp, original response and any translation, report-status tag, substantive code, observed behaviour, supporting/conflicting evidence and an analytic memo. Adapt the fields to the design rather than treating this as a mandatory coding template. Retain cross-source disagreement rather than resolving it automatically in favour of the interview or recording.

Before substantive coding, distinguish reported event-time recall, present-day reflection/hindsight, uncertain or mixed temporal status, and explicit non-recall. Keep unelicited, missing or unintelligible material distinguishable from non-recall when relevant. Explain the categories and ambiguity handling; these are practical analytic distinctions, not a universal validated scale. They describe the status participants present in their accounts, rather than verifying recovery of original thoughts. Mixed accounts may remain indeterminate.

Specify the coding unit, event/case boundaries, codebook or interpretive procedure, examples and consequential revisions. For a codebook/content-analysis team, independently code a stated subset and report disagreements and their resolution; choose any agreement statistic and unit because they fit the design. Do not impose inter-rater coefficients on every qualitative approach or relabel a coding-reliability pipeline as reflexive thematic analysis.

Where counts are used, report both coded occurrences and distinct contributing participants, with explicit denominators and the nesting of repeated reports within episodes and people. Thirty mentions do not imply thirty participants. Make clear whether a percentage refers to turns, episodes, coded occurrences or participants, and how overlapping codes are counted. Code frequencies alone do not establish theme importance or population prevalence.

## GenAI and eye-tracking boundaries

Link a feedback episode to the output actually displayed, the participant's subsequent action/revision and their recall account. A fixation or pause alone does not establish noticing, comprehension, trust, cognitive load or learning. Explain how gaze is processed, synchronised and used as a cue. A gaze overlay can direct attention during the interview; justify and pilot it, and distinguish original viewing behaviour from attention created by replay.

An absent report does not prove that a process was absent. Feedback uptake or a revision does not itself establish learning. Stronger learning or causal claims require appropriate outcomes, timing and a design that supports them. Keep observation, reported experience, engagement, uptake and learning conceptually distinct.

## Ethics and reporting

Obtain the applicable approval and consent for recording, replay, transcription, reuse and excerpt publication. Address third-party and minor privacy, voluntariness and teacher–student power. Minimise unrelated screen/chat capture, pseudonymise identifiers and use controlled storage. External AI transcription or analysis needs separately established permission and appropriate data governance; consent to an interview does not automatically cover it.

Report the RQ and rationale, participant/task context, cue/event selection, actual delays and intervening activity, instructions, playback/pause control, prompts, interviewer role, language/translation, analysis units and procedures, missing/non-recall material, triangulation and disagreement, reflexivity and inference limits. Provide the actual instructions and prompts in an appendix when the requested output permits. Apply the relevant APRIL JARS qualitative or mixed reporting routes after the methodological decisions; do not treat reporting completeness as validity.
