# Developing a study-specific theoretical framework

## Source and scope

Lederman, N. G., & Lederman, J. S. (2015). What is a theoretical framework? A practical answer. *Journal of Science Teacher Education, 26*, 593–597. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10972-015-9443-2

This five-page editorial offers methodological guidance and a hypothetical illustration. It does not empirically validate a framework or supply findings that can replace the original empirical studies. The interpretation below follows the article's printed pages; APRIL's practical workflow is identified separately.

## Working definition

A theoretical framework is the literature-grounded argument that conceptualises and justifies a particular investigation: it establishes the problem or question and explains why the proposed approach offers a defensible way to address it. It may use an established theory but does not necessarily require one.

Read this as a broad, study-specific justification. The framework connects what the literature establishes, what remains uncertain, the concepts or assumptions needed to investigate it, and the reasoning behind the proposed questions and approach. A pre-existing named theory is one possible resource for that argument.

## What the editorial argues

- **Questions and rationale (p. 594):** identify the problem/question and explain why the proposed approach is feasible. In the illustration, feasibility concerns the intellectual rationale for investigating the problem, beyond access to time, participants or resources.
- **Literature grounding (pp. 593–595):** review theoretical and empirical scholarship thoroughly, including apparent gaps. Explain how that scholarship supports the particular investigation. An apparent absence of studies requires verification and a reason the missing knowledge matters.
- **Methodological breadth (pp. 593, 595–597):** avoid forcing a named formal theory onto inductive, descriptive, correlational or ethnographic inquiry. Such studies still require a reasoned framing and significance. Hypotheses suit some theory-testing studies; they are not a universal requirement.
- **Timing and terminology (pp. 593, 596):** distinguish a theory that guides initial design from a theory applied later during analysis. The editorial explicitly postpones the theoretical-versus-conceptual distinction; it establishes neither a universal dichotomy nor universal equivalence between those terms.

### The hypothetical example (pp. 594–595)

The authors describe inconsistent findings about teachers' questions at different cognitive levels and student achievement. That inconsistency establishes a problem to explain. Literature on cognitive development offers a possible account of why the effects might differ and motivates more focused questions or hypotheses. The point is the connection from literature to a justified investigation. This is a hypothetical illustration, not a demonstrated explanation or evidence that those relationships hold. Consult the original studies before making empirical claims about them.

Do not turn every contextual claim in the editorial into a rule. In particular, do not impose its quantitative/realist–qualitative/idealist generalisation or its discussion of bracketing and Wolcott's validity position as universal qualitative requirements. Follow the actual study's paradigm and appropriate methodological sources.

## APRIL workflow: derived operationalisation

The checks below are APRIL's operationalisation of the editorial's argument, not its verbatim checklist or a mandatory linear sequence. Refine provisional questions and the search iteratively as the evidence develops.

1. Clarify the phenomenon, context, purpose and provisional research question; preserve established decisions and ask only for missing information that affects the framework.
2. Synthesise relevant empirical and theoretical literature by what it establishes, contests or leaves uncertain. Verify citations and the role of each source.
3. Identify a supported problem, inconsistency, uncertainty or gap and explain its significance. A gap alone does not demonstrate importance.
4. Define the concepts, assumptions and distinctions needed for this investigation. Select a named theory only when its explanatory or interpretive work is clear and appropriate.
5. Explain how the selected evidence and concepts justify the RQs and proposed approach. Show why this perspective fits the problem and acknowledge consequential alternatives or limits.
6. Develop propositions or hypotheses only when warranted by the purpose, literature and design. Preserve openness in inductive work; do not predetermine themes or impose unsupported directional/causal expectations.
7. Check alignment across the problem, concepts, RQs, design and intended interpretation. Distinguish established findings, assumptions, proposed relationships and unresolved questions; correlations alone do not establish causation.
8. Write a connected study-specific argument. Add an optional diagram only after the prose logic is clear, using supported relationships and explicit labels for tentative links. The figure summarises the argument.

## Deliverable and integrity checks

For a framework request, provide coherent prose explaining the investigation's problem and rationale, key concepts and supported relationships/assumptions where applicable, why the selected perspective fits, and how it connects to the RQs and approach. Include accurate citations and evidence limits; flag proposed or unverified elements. Remain within the requested deliverable and preserve the existing manuscript unless edits are authorised.

As an APRIL quality check, a citation list, theory catalogue, diagram or taxonomy of dimensions/competencies alone is insufficient. For a literacy or competency model assembled from several sources, explain how the domains were synthesised and why they justify this investigation; the number of citations does not itself establish a theory. Use terminology appropriate to the field without claiming that this editorial resolves the theoretical/conceptual distinction. Do not invent theories, citations, empirical gaps or causal relationships, and do not add arbitrary boxes/arrows to create the appearance of a framework.
